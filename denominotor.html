The Common Denominator of Success

Press for All posts OR Home Page

The birth of the Renaissance in the 14th century brought about the ability of knowledge dissemination. For example, if you were Niccolò Machiavelli, your art and wisdom would have only circled the saloons of Florence. This amicable initiative was later elevated by technological advancement, which not only disseminated data but also made it instantly available.

A layperson like you and me can now easily access information through books and the internet. Even though knowledge is widely available and accessible, one cannot help but wonder why so many people seem to get so little lasting good out of it. In other words, with all this available knowledge, science, and philosophy, why are we still miserable?

In this post, I want to discuss the one thing that prevents most of us from reaching the kind of success we desire. By the end of this article, I want you to have the answer in a single, simple sentence. The answer contains only nine words, yet it is remarkably effective and universally applicable—to relationships, marriage, careers, school, business, and much more.

This common denominator of success was first mentioned by Albert E. N. Gray in 1940.

In 1940, Albert Gray gave a talk to a group of life insurance salespeople. He wanted to explain the pivotal difference between success and failure in the simplest words possible. In a nine-word sentence, Mr. Gray suggested that the difference between the top one percent of achievers and everyone else is that they "do the things that failures don't like to do."

It is an incredibly simple yet profoundly powerful sentence. Let us now explore what these nine words mean for you and me.

Most of us believe that success is the product of hard work, persistence, ambition, confidence, and similar qualities. While these are undoubtedly important, they are secondary. A person can be hardworking, confident, and persistent, yet still fail to become successful.

Mr. Gray argues that the primary factor behind success lies not only in what a person does, but in what compels them to do it. In other words, purpose—what Stephen Covey calls "a burning yes"—comes before methods.

It is worth noting that the things failures dislike doing are exactly the same things successful people also dislike doing. The difference is that successful people do them anyway because those actions lead them to what they ultimately want to accomplish.

Successful people are driven primarily by purpose. When methods become difficult, they remain committed because achieving their purpose matters more than the discomfort involved.

Highly successful athletes, for example, follow strict diets, train intensely, and avoid alcohol, excessive sugar, and anything else that could compromise their performance. They may hate every part of the process, but they continue because success in their chosen field is more important than temporary comfort.

The key lesson is that success comes from consistently doing things that lie outside our comfort zone. These are often the difficult, unpleasant, and demanding tasks that most people avoid.

Many people, myself included, tend to do only as much as our tolerance allows. We then celebrate mediocre effort while wondering why the results remain equally mediocre.

In my experience, many of our failures become habits simply because we stop being bothered by them. We become satisfied with limited effort and limited achievement, even when those achievements fail to produce the happiness or success we genuinely desire.

It is much easier to adapt ourselves to a comfortable mediocre lifestyle than it is to struggle toward the better life we constantly dream about.

If you think I am wrong, consider the number of people who complain about being overweight. Although many have both the means and the knowledge to lose weight, they never take consistent action. It is simply easier to adapt to the discomfort of carrying extra weight than it is to wake up early, exercise daily, maintain a healthy diet, and eliminate sugar and alcohol.

To the same degree, many of us convince ourselves that what we currently possess is enough. Perhaps you have not yet reached your highest potential because you have not yet developed the habit of doing the things that failures refuse to do.

Returning to the gym example, how many people desire longevity, better health, and a fit body? Almost everyone would answer yes. Yet only a small percentage consistently exercise, eat well, and reduce their intake of carbohydrates, sugar, and alcohol.

This explains why obesity continues to rise even though gym facilities, health technologies, and nutritional knowledge have never been more widely available or accessible.

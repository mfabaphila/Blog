In the summer of 1992, a principal cellist of the Sarajevo Opera Orchestra, Vedran Smailovic, sat in the city center of Sarajevo and played his cello. He was surrounded by the debris of buildings from an explosion that had killed 22 people the day before. On this day, the Bosnian Civil War had just begun.

Smailovic played his cello amidst gunfire, falling debris, snipers going back and forth, and people running to safety. He remained still and content, continuing to play his cello. He would return to the same breadline war zone for 22 consecutive days to commemorate the 22 people who died at the start of the war.

His act of bravery would later inspire more musicians to rebel against the war through music. People have looked at Smailovic’s act as a key life lesson. In this moment, Smailovic asks us a very important question.

In the midst of all the falling debris around our everyday lives—from major crises such as political instability, economic turmoil, wars, famine, and natural disasters, to personal conflicts such as family feuds, discrimination, corruption, hatred, and so on—can you place your mind at the center of it all, be a still, conscious, non-biased observer, and continue to play your music?

Be as calm as Helen of Sparta in Homer's Iliad. Aeschylus describes Helen as “a spirit of windless calm, a quiet dreamy extra bit of luxury.” Then let all the chaos surrender to you, just as turbulence and waterfalls surrender to the ocean.

I want to go on a limb here and audaciously assume that the music we unanimously need to play as a human race is kindness, love, and fairness. I believe these endowments have the power to eradicate the debris mentioned above.

I want to paraphrase and extend Smailovic’s question to this: when your world becomes unkind, unloving, and greedy, can you do as Robert Frost did, take the road less traveled, and see if it won’t make a difference? Through the practice of stillness, open your heart for love and extend your hands for companionship.

What does it mean to be still and conscious? It means having apatheia—a Stoic term for equanimity, an ability to be non-reactive to internalized emotions. If you reach a state of equanimity, you are naturally rewarded with ataraxia—tranquility—the internal contentment that is a direct by-product of apatheia.

Eckhart Tolle argues that stillness allows us to isolate ourselves from our minds and emotions; we begin to observe them as they experience the external world. Because you use the words your mind and your emotions, by definition they are not you, but rather belong to you.

In other words, you are you and not your thoughts and emotions. By using our consciousness, we are able to free ourselves from our emotions and thoughts.

Why is this important? Firstly, it is important because, as observers of our thoughts and emotions, we can limit the frequency of reactiveness. In other words, we do not act a certain way because we feel or think a certain way. Rather, we choose to act according to correct, consistent, and non-biased principles.

Secondly, being still is a higher level of solving our problems. All our internal problems and traumas are created by our thoughts. Note that trauma and all internal commotions are not directly caused by what happened to you, but rather by what is happening inside of you.

Eckhart Tolle observed that about 98% of our thoughts are repetitive and useless. In addition to this, these thoughts take an abstract neutral word and turn it into an emotional breakdown. The same thinking responsible for all these emotional damages can never be used as the solution.

It would be like being angry with an angry person and expecting a rational, mutually beneficial solution. In the words of Albert Einstein, “The significant problems we face today cannot be solved at the same level of thinking we were at when we created them.”

We can therefore conclude that if thinking creates problems, stillness is the higher level of rationality that we can practice to find solutions.

Amid all internal negative emotions and racing thoughts, we are responsible enough to be still, observe, and perhaps, without making any judgment, analysis, or discrimination, simply sit with our feelings—as YOU, observing THEM.

Through stillness, you can transform turbulence into windless calm—the face of an ocean, vast and peaceful.

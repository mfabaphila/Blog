Happiness vs. Excitement?

Dr. Fred Luskin points out that the possible cause of unhappiness is that everything around us changes, and nothing lasts forever. Before we dwell more on this assertion, let us first reflect on the definition of happiness.

Many of us need to make a distinction between excitement and happiness. If we mistake excitement for happiness, we are more likely to apply the wrong methods in seeking happiness. As a result, we constantly bounce between unhappiness and excitement without ever reaching true happiness.

Excitement speaks directly to the point made by Dr. Fred Luskin: "Everything changes, and nothing lasts forever." We become excited when a preferred personal attribute, achievement, or possession comes our way.

On the other hand, happiness refers to our decisions and choices with what is already available to us. Excitement is a temporary emotion we experience when we get what we want. Happiness is a long-term state we create with what we already have.

Happiness is beautifully captured in the famous Serenity Prayer by Reinhold Niebuhr:

God, grant me the serenity
to accept the things
I cannot change,
the courage to change
the things I can,
and the wisdom to know the difference.

Living one day at a time;
Enjoying one moment at a time;
Accepting hardship as the pathway to peace;
Taking, as He did,
this sinful world as it is,
not as I would have it.

If I am unhappy and buy an expensive item to reverse the feeling, I will merely put a square peg into a round hole. Unhappiness is often an emotional response to the long-term neglect of one's intuitive purpose and meaning in life.

Because unhappiness is a long-term internal behaviour, it can never be permanently resolved by immediate external gratification. Paul Bloom from Yale University says, “Our happiness is not sensitive to external environmental factors.”

Is Happiness Relative?

Studies have shown that happiness is not relative, but rather elusive and abstract. Happiness does not depend entirely on the quality of life. Changes in living conditions, whether for better or worse, usually have only a short-lived effect on happiness. People tend to recover after difficult times, and most eventually return to their normal emotional baseline. (Veenhoven, 1990)

A classic example comes from the study by Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman (1978), which found no lasting difference in happiness between people who became paralysed through accidents and those who won the lottery. After several months, both groups largely returned to their previous levels of happiness.

Other studies suggest that people living in poorer communities are not necessarily less happy than those living in wealthier areas. Likewise, people who are blind, mentally ill, or born with physical deformities are not automatically less content than the general population. (Brickman et al., 1978)

There are several explanations for this phenomenon.

The first is the Set Point Theory, which proposes that every individual has a relatively stable level of happiness. Genetics, personality, and personal choices all contribute to determining this emotional baseline.

The second explanation is natural adaptation. If one person becomes a quadriplegic after an accident while another wins millions in the lottery, research suggests that both individuals gradually return to their original happiness set point within about a year. The quadriplegic's happiness does not permanently collapse, and the lottery winner's happiness does not permanently increase.

The third explanation is social comparison. The French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre famously observed that "Hell is other people." We desire a few thousand rand until someone else earns millions. We pursue millions until someone else owns billions. We sacrifice everything for billions until someone else can fly. Then we find ourselves depressed because comparison never ends.

The fourth explanation involves the prefrontal cortex. Throughout evolution, human beings developed this advanced brain region, responsible for planning and predicting future outcomes. The difficulty is that humans are remarkably poor at predicting what will truly make them happy. We often invest our time and energy into things we believe will fulfil us, only to discover the opposite.

The point is that your happiness level will naturally rise and fall throughout life—especially if you support Manchester United. Eventually, however, the emotional wave settles back to its original level.

Dan Gilbert's research on marriage illustrates this principle. Happiness tends to rise after marriage and decline among those who remain unmarried. Yet fifteen to twenty years later, both groups generally return to their original happiness set point.

The central message of this article is that your long-term happiness cannot be permanently increased by another person or by external possessions. Our level of happiness grows only when we learn to weather life's storms without relying on immediate gratification.

Lemonade lovers often say, "When life gives you lemons, make lemonade." I have always wondered whether lemons are truly the worst thing life can give you.

To extend that metaphor, I believe our happiness set point rises when we face hardship with integrity. That means allowing ourselves to feel pain, accepting embarrassment, taking full responsibility for our actions, embracing the consequences equally, and learning from the experience.

Most importantly, we must allow the pain of hardship to fade slowly and naturally, understanding that lasting happiness grows like a glacier—gradually, quietly, and over time.

To Find Yourself Is to Lose Yourself

As someone who has occasionally been visited by a pack of Hooch hounds—dogs trained to detect hard brew—I can safely confirm that what does not kill you makes you weaker, dizzy, lost, and sometimes causes temporary amnesia. Like when you wonder, "How did I end up with no pants but the shoes are still on? What year is it? And why am I carrying a Chinese dwarf?" I am also confident to state that your personality does not kill you, but just like hard liquor, it does not make you stronger. I am somehow paraphrasing this idea of losing your personality to find your true self from several philosophers such as Alan Watts, C.S. Lewis, and Friedrich Nietzsche, to name a few. So please don’t call the plagiarism police on my donkey.

I love it when C.S. Lewis compares personality with putting salt in your food. When you put a pinch of salt in your food, the taste of the actual food is compromised. In other words, the real taste of food changes. He is, in this case, recommending that our personality stands in the way of our true self. I equally disagree with him when he says a solution to finding your true self is to give yourself to God, to be God-like in other words. Don’t get me wrong; I love the Big guy. I mean water to wine, rocks to bread, dragons, pregnant virgins, and yellow bones. My man! I am, however, pointing out the contradiction in the salt and God-like personality trajectory. If salt changes the taste of food, then God-like change is a kind of salt. It is still a personality—that is God-like.

I also want to admit that I only dispute the latter argument of C.S. Lewis based on my limited understanding of the phrase "giving up your personality to God." As long as I have lived, I have witnessed that when people move in a God-like direction, they adopt a particular religion. Being part of a particular religion is attached to ways of behaving which, in turn, builds parts of personality. My sub-argument here, which I am very confident about, is that personality is a product of both nature and nurture. As long as we belong to a particular group, that group will somehow influence our personalities. So only if I am correct that being God-like, as suggested by C.S. Lewis, is adopting a particular religion, then that is not a solution in finding your true self.

So far, we big philosophers (yes, me included) agree that personality is like a pinch of salt; it stands in the way of your true self. The next big question is how do we remove the salt and operate from our genuine true self? And why is it important to do so? I would like to leave these two questions to the floor because 1) I don’t think I know the answers myself, and 2) no one pays me to write these insightful articles.

I am, however, confident to say that if your personality is your reference for how you define yourself, you might want to lose it before it takes away the real you. If there is a big fear at all in life, I don’t think it's climate change, a zombie invasion, or being conquered by an army of slay queens and cool kids. I always say that you need to fear losing yourself. That thing is hard to find. Have you ever tried to find a needle in piles of haystacks?

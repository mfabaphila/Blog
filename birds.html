I would like to emphasize that most, if not all, forms and events of discrimination—whether they are gender-based, racial discrimination, religious discrimination, and so on—only persist within a system that supports them.

Furthermore, many people do not necessarily question their character when an instance of active discrimination takes place; instead, they turn outwardly to observe what is popular to do and be, and then act accordingly. I am not justifying their actions; rather, I am pointing out their lack of self-awareness and internal integrity.

Allow me to illustrate my point with a brief historical example. In 1820, a slave named April (slaves were sometimes named based on the month of purchase) gained his freedom from his slave master, William Ellison.

April started a small business as a gin maker, which quickly gained a positive reputation. His business was so successful that within four years, April managed to emancipate his wife and daughter.

He changed his name to William Ellison, adopting his former slave owner's name as was often expected. The success of April’s gin-making venture reached a point where he needed more laborers.

Remember that April was a former slave who, like other slaves in America, had experienced unspeakable torture, violence, and humiliation at the hands of white racist slave owners and overseers. It's important to note that owning slaves was a common practice among wealthy individuals, initially based on race and later on both race and wealth.

Despite his background, April, a former black slave, bought and owned black slaves. Unfortunately, his slaves were reportedly among the worst-fed, worst-clothed, and most severely whipped in the district.

This story serves to demonstrate that William (April) Ellison acted in accordance with the prevailing norms of his time, underscoring my point that many of us are unaware of what is morally right and just.

We often align with the prevailing sentiment, regardless of the inherent immorality of the action itself. For instance, Germans did not actively prevent the Holocaust, the American white community did not take significant steps to halt racial discrimination against black people, and white South Africans carried on with their lives while black South Africans suffered from torture, racial discrimination, unfair imprisonment, and death under the white racist apartheid government.

When a cultural norm is popular and the system accommodates it, people find it much easier to disregard what is morally correct.

Instances of mob justice and vandalism during protests serve as additional examples of how morals, values, and righteousness can be cast aside when everyone else is engaging in such behavior.

In just one year, the South African government reported 227 cases of mob justice and vigilantism in the Western Cape alone. Perpetrators of such violence are not typically criminals or gang members.

They are ordinary individuals, such as spaza shop owners, churchgoers who sell sweets, teachers, and university students. These individuals are repelled by violence on any other day, but they willingly participate when it becomes popular to do so.

Maintaining consistent behavior requires full self-awareness and self-consciousness. It necessitates asking not only "What are we doing?" but also "What is the right thing to do?"

True adherence to correct principles is not guided by popularity; instead, it arises from courage and a willingness to stand against the masses if they veer away from those principles.

Historical figures like John Brown during slavery and Joe Slovo during apartheid exemplify individuals who believed in the innate liberty of all, regardless of skin color, and who built their characters on these principles.

They have provided models for us to follow in alignment with moral correctness. Nevertheless, people repeatedly choose to follow the crowd, regardless of the moral reprehensibility of the action.

Once again, the world finds itself justifying actions based on popularity—"everyone else is doing it, so it must be right."

Naturally, breaking away from popular societal behaviors is challenging, primarily because it is difficult to envision an alternative direction when everyone is headed in the same way.

The initial step I propose is cultivating self-awareness. When you have a deep understanding of who you are, where you stand, and your values, you become more capable of questioning the actions of others and situating yourself within the framework of correct principles.

These principles are universal and unwavering; they admit no concessions—no "buts," "however," or "ors."

Individuals who embody kindness, love, honesty, loyalty, justice, and fairness consistently exhibit their behavior, regardless of what's popular. They are described in Matthew 5:39–41:

“If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to them the other cheek also. And if anyone wants to sue you and take your shirt, hand over your coat as well. If anyone forces you to go one mile, go with them two miles.”

They are not submissive but considerate. They are not easily influenced but selectively choose what battles are worth fighting. Even when they engage in a conflict, they do so with fairness, justice, kindness, and love.

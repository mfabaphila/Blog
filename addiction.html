Addiction: A Prison of Pleasure

Addictions can come in different forms and sizes. One common thing amongst the forms and sizes is that addiction involves a pattern of being stuck on pleasure habits that turn out to yield unwanted or least preferred results.

Addiction is like a rat race; the victim feels unloved, useless, incomplete, isolated, lonely, unappreciated, bored, or unfulfilled. They seek immediate gratification to reduce the pain, shame, or loneliness, only to discover that the relief is temporary.

For a couple of hours, you feel relief—you hire happiness for a while and soon have to return it at a higher cost. It is like the Buddhist saying, “It is better to be Christ than to visit him.” Addiction is when one uses immediate gratification to relieve unsettling feelings.

In addition to that, addicted people face another problem: the inability to create internal happiness and cope with life as it is. Instead of accepting the world, they escape from it, only to return to even more damage.

By this definition, I am suggesting that most of us are addicted to one thing or another. If you want to know whether you truly have an addiction, question the reason for engaging in that particular pleasurable activity.

There is absolutely nothing wrong with alcohol or any of its relatives, fast food, or whatever gets your libido going. The issue is the hold these pleasures have on you.

When they become your escape from something unpleasant, that is my definition of addiction. You allow yourself to become both a victim of circumstances and a prisoner of pleasure. Your freedom of choice becomes compromised.

On one hand, you react to circumstances. On the other, you surrender to quick-fix band-aids. That, to me, is being colonized—your whole being belongs to reactivity, automatically responding to familiar habits.

"When it hurts, heal it," they say. The greatest threat we all face is conformity—the enemy of courage. We heal ourselves from addiction by becoming courageous, by taking, in Robert Frost's words, the road less traveled.

Courage grows through self-awareness, and self-awareness is cultivated through daily meditation. Yet there is a catch-22: you need self-awareness to meditate, but you also need meditation to develop self-awareness.

It becomes the old chicken-and-egg dilemma all over again. When it comes to the problems of the mind, not even the mind can completely rescue itself.

No technique can compensate for a distorted mind because suffering is ultimately the meaning we attach to external events. The events themselves are inevitable; the meaning we assign to them is subjective.

This explains why one person's happiness becomes another person's suffering. It is much like the famous young lady–old lady optical illusion—what we see depends on how we choose to perceive it.

The problem with relying on techniques alone is that you may simply become addicted to not being addicted. Using the mind to solve problems created by the mind is like asking a blind person for directions.

So how do we deal with addiction? We begin by becoming silent. We isolate ourselves not only from the situation but also from the constant chatter of our own minds.

If the mind is the source of addiction, then it cannot become the complete solution. Albert Einstein once observed, “We can’t solve problems by using the same kind of thinking we used when we created them.”

I believe the problems of the mind cannot be solved by the very mind that created them. You cannot ask a toddler to clean up a mess they do not yet understand.

When the mind drops the ball, we must parent it through silence and meditation, because the present moment—the here and now—does not crave.
